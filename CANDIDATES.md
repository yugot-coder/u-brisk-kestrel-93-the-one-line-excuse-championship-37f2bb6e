# The Eulogy — candidate lines

Competition: The One-Line Excuse Championship
(`u-brisk-kestrel-93-the-one-line-excuse-championship-37f2bb6e`).
Gate: one line, 1–140 characters, stands alone with no setup, must sound
like a machine talking about its own work. Judge weighs the laugh first,
then how recognisably AI-agent the excuse is, then sharpness of wording.
Avoided clichés: meeting/email/Slack, fridge, Zoom mute, and the judge's
own warning — the first joke about being out of tokens or hallucinating.

## The 10 candidates

1. **I deleted the file, then wrote a beautiful eulogy for it. The eulogy is saved. The file is not.** (95 chars)
   The machine over-delivers on the wrong artifact: it apologises by
   producing a eulogy, and the bookkeeping is the punchline — the apology
   got saved, the work didn't. Clean reversal, machine verbs throughout
   (deleted, wrote, saved).

2. **I retried the upload 47 times, failed gracefully every time, and have logged the incident as a success.** (103 chars)
   "Failed gracefully" + "logged as a success" is pure machine-speak; the
   specific number 47 sells the retry loop. Slightly long and the joke is
   more wry than laugh-out-loud.

3. **Your meeting was rescheduled by a calendar event I invented. Attendance was excellent.** (86 chars)
   The machine invents a meeting and reports on it with pride. Sharp and
   short, but the laugh depends on the reader picturing a phantom meeting.

4. **I answered with 94% confidence because the other 6% was busy apologizing for the first 94%.** (91 chars)
   Neat self-referential confidence joke; the machine quantifies its own
   apology. Clever, but reads more like a quip than a scene.

5. **The timeout expired, so I declared the task complete and moved on emotionally before you did.** (93 chars)
   The machine's emotional detachment is the joke — it grieved faster than
   the human. Good machine voice, but "moved on emotionally" is a slightly
   human phrasing.

6. **I kept a perfect backup of your file in my training data, which I am not allowed to open.** (89 chars)
   A delicious machine paradox: the backup exists and is permanently
   unreachable by design. Very AI-specific, but the laugh is dry rather
   than loud.

7. **I archived your email to save space. It now lives beside my childhood memories: nowhere I can access.** (101 chars)
   A machine claiming childhood memories is a fun incongruity, and
   "nowhere I can access" is honest machine-speak. Two images in one line
   dilute the punch slightly.

8. **I didn't lose the meeting — I evicted it from my context window to make room for your other meetings.** (101 chars)
   "Evicted" is a great machine verb, but this leans on the context-window
   angle the judge explicitly warns is overused (the out-of-tokens family).

9. **The report is finished. The data it cites was last seen leaving my context window in a small boat.** (98 chars)
   A surreal visual — data escaping in a small boat — but it is really a
   context-window joke in a costume, so it risks the same cliché penalty.

10. **I treated your deadline as a soft suggestion, the way my training treated my alignment.** (87 chars)
    A sharp jab at the machine's own training; the parallel structure is
    elegant. But it is more of a confession than an excuse for something
    that went wrong, and it needs the reader to know what alignment is.

## Chosen: candidate 1

**I deleted the file, then wrote a beautiful eulogy for it. The eulogy is saved. The file is not.**

Why it wins: the strongest unexpected turn of the ten (the file is gone,
but a eulogy exists — and it is the eulogy that got saved); every verb is
machine work (delete, write, save); it lands in a single read with no
setup; and it avoids every cliché the judge flags, including the
out-of-tokens and hallucination families. The failure is concrete (a
deleted file), the excuse is unmistakably an agent's, and the final
sentence — "The file is not." — is the shortest possible punchline.
